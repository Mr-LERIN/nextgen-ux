Developers Day — Apple WWDC 2026 Day 2

The keynote gets the headlines. Day 2 is where WWDC does its real work.

If Day 1 of WWDC 2026 was Apple making promises to consumers, Day 2 was Apple showing developers how to keep them.

The Monday keynote told the world what was coming. Tuesday’s Platforms State of the Union — a longer, denser, significantly nerdier session — told the engineers who build on Apple’s platforms exactly how to build with what was announced.

Foundation Models: Rebuilt From the Ground Up

The headline from Day 2 for developers was direct: Apple’s Foundation Models framework has been rebuilt entirely, in collaboration with Google, leveraging technology from the Gemini family of models.

This isn’t just a performance upgrade. The framework now supports image input — developers can prompt on-device models with images as well as text, enabling apps to pull text and QR codes out of images locally, without sending data to the cloud. It also now supports cloud model integration, allowing developers to connect to any cloud model provider when tasks exceed what on-device processing can handle.

The most significant new tool is Dynamic Profiles — a system that lets apps swap models, tools, and instructions on the fly so behavior can adapt within a continuous session. Apple also confirmed that the Foundation Models framework will be open-sourced later this year.

Core AI: Apple Silicon’s New Native Layer

Introduced quietly but with significant long-term implications: Core AI, an entirely new framework built into the operating system, designed specifically to run on-device models and take full advantage of Apple Silicon.

Where Foundation Models is the high-level interface, Core AI is the infrastructure underneath. It’s Apple’s bet that as AI features become standard across apps, the performance ceiling should be the chip itself — not software overhead.

App Intents and the Agentic Siri

The other major developer story was the updated App Intents framework — the bridge that connects third-party apps to Siri AI’s new agentic capabilities.

For Siri to do things inside your apps, those apps need to expose what they can do. App Intents is the mechanism for that. Apple updated it to support entity schemas, intent schemas, and view annotations, giving developers the tools to make their content discoverable and their actions available through Siri via natural language.

The practical result: if a developer properly implements App Intents, a user can ask Siri to perform a task inside that app — book a table, log a workout, send a payment — without ever opening the app manually.

Liquid Glass, SwiftUI, and Xcode

Apple addressed developer feedback on Liquid Glass. The default setting is now more legible over complex backgrounds, better calibrated for readability, more personalizable, and more consistent across apps.

SwiftUI received meaningful updates: drag-to-reorder and swipe actions now work in any container, nested layouts resize up to twice as fast, and automatic async image caching is built in. Xcode updates focused on making it easier to build, test, and ship apps for the full range of new platforms.

What Day 2 Tells Us

The Platforms State of the Union is always a signal about what Apple actually wants to happen. This year’s signal was consistent: Apple wants developers to treat AI as infrastructure, not a feature.

Core AI runs at the OS level. Foundation Models is being open-sourced. App Intents is the integration layer for Siri’s new intelligence. The bet Apple is making is that if developers build deeply into these frameworks, the entire ecosystem benefits — and so does Siri’s ability to act across every app on your phone.
