AI Just Outdiagnosed the ER Doctor?

A Harvard trial showed AI correctly diagnosed 67% of emergency patients, compared to 50–55% by triage doctors.

A study published in Science from a research team at Harvard Medical School and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center dropped a number that stopped a lot of people mid-scroll: OpenAI’s o1 reasoning model had correctly diagnosed 67% of emergency room patients compared to 50 to 55% by triage doctors. That gap is not small. In emergency medicine, where minutes and correct assessments change outcomes, a 12 to 17 percentage point accuracy difference is significant by any measure.

This is the number healthcare AI advocates have been pointing toward for years. And it deserves more than a headline — it deserves a conversation about what comes next.

What the Study Actually Shows

Context makes this finding even more powerful. Triage nurses and doctors working in the ER operate under conditions that no unassisted system can match: noise, stress, incomplete patient histories, language barriers, a packed waiting room, and the relentless pressure of too many patients and not enough hands. AI stepping in to support that environment isn’t a threat to clinicians — it’s exactly what they’ve needed.

A 67% diagnostic accuracy rate in that setting is extraordinary. It means AI is performing at its highest when clinicians are stretched at their thinnest — covering the gap that understaffed, under-resourced emergency departments have struggled with for decades.

AI isn’t coming for the clinician’s job. It’s coming for the burnout, the backlogs, and the diagnostic gaps that no single human should have to carry alone.

The Clinician-AI Partnership Is the Point

Having worked in clinical environments, there are things that happen in an assessment that no text input fully captures. The way a patient holds their arm. The subtle pallor that shows up under fluorescent lights. The hesitation when you ask about pain level. The instinct that something systemic is happening before any lab value confirms it. These are real clinical signals — and they are precisely what makes the clinician irreplaceable.

But here’s what that means: AI and the bedside nurse aren’t competing. They’re complementary. AI brings pattern recognition across millions of cases at scale. The nurse brings present, human judgment. Together, they are more accurate, more attentive, and more capable than either operating alone.

Where AI Is Already Transforming Healthcare

The wins are already here. Clinical note summarization: AI can reduce a 30-page patient history to a decision-relevant summary in seconds. Drug interaction checking: AI flags combinations a fatigued pharmacist might miss at the end of a 12-hour shift. Imaging analysis: AI-assisted radiology is matching or exceeding specialist accuracy in several domains. Documentation: AI is slashing the charting burden that has been burning out nurses and doctors for years.

What the 67% Number Should Inspire

This is the moment to accelerate, not hesitate. We should be asking: how do we get AI-assisted triage into every under-resourced emergency department in the world? How do we build systems that give rural hospitals the diagnostic support that only large urban centers currently have access to? How do we use this technology to close the health equity gaps that have persisted for generations?

The answer isn’t to slow down and wait for perfect conditions. The answer is to build thoughtfully, deploy responsibly, and keep clinicians at the center of every decision — not because AI can’t be trusted, but because the human-AI partnership is more powerful than either alone.

The 67% headline isn’t a warning. It’s an invitation.
